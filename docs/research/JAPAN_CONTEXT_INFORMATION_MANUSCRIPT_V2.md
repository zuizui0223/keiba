# Individual histories reshape the predictive value of family records in managed environmental allocation

**Manuscript v2 · 26 November 2026 · draft, not submitted.** Authors, affiliations, journal, ethics statement and source-use permissions require confirmation. All analyses are retrospective. This version separates the principal evidence from exploratory boundary tests; no new model fitting or new empirical sample was performed.

## Abstract

Records of relatives may help predict an individual whose own history is poorly observed, yet the same records can become redundant as individual histories accumulate. We asked how this additional predictive value changes within individuals and how changing observation membership modifies the apparent population pattern. We used Japanese Thoroughbred racing as a managed system, measuring environmental allocation as the fraction of classified turf rather than dirt starts within a reference distance band. Earlier same-sire records improved annual allocation prediction in 2021 (7,276 horses; mean squared error reduction 0.01471). Equalizing paternal and maternal donor counts reduced their observed prediction gap by 86%, without establishing a remaining directional advantage. Among 599 horses observed in both 2022 and 2024, the gain from a fixed family correction changed from +0.01171 to −0.01587. Updating individual histories accounted for −0.01611 of this change (conditional pointwise 95% sire-resampling interval −0.02506 to −0.00778); the observed allocation shift accounted for −0.01147. Changing membership offset 38% of the common-cohort decline. In a matched subset, the direction of recorded-sire corrections aligned more closely with individual-history updates than did magnitude-matched alternative-sire directions. History-dependent weights estimated from earlier records reduced excess correction, but their gain over the individual baseline was uncertain in 2024 and a season-matched 2025 extension. These results distinguish family-associated resemblance, incremental predictive usefulness and observational composition. They show why a population-level prediction trend need not describe a within-individual change, without identifying genetic causation, animal learning or voluntary habitat preference.

**Keywords:** conditional predictive information; individual history; family records; realized environmental allocation; observation membership; longitudinal comparison

## Introduction

Ecological prediction often draws on information from repeated observations of a focal individual and from records of other individuals. The usefulness of these sources depends on their overlap as well as their precision. Combining family and individual records is not a new idea: animal breeding has long developed selection indices and evaluated parental and ancestral records as information for genetic merit (Hazel 1943; Young 1961; Searle 1963). Our target is different. We assess the prediction of a realized environmental allocation, not breeding value, and ask how the incremental value of a fixed family-indexed correction changes on the same repeatedly observed individuals when their own records become more extensive.

A complementary issue arises in longitudinal ecology: comparisons between observations at different times need not represent changes within individuals because the individuals represented may also change (van de Pol & Verhulst 2006; van de Pol & Wright 2009). Observation processes can affect both inference and prediction (Chadwick et al. 2024). We extend this perspective to the value of an auxiliary prediction channel. Rather than assuming that a later decrease in average prediction gain indicates the declining usefulness of the channel within individuals, we distinguish the contributions of changed focal predictors, changed observed outcomes and changed membership. This decomposition is an arithmetic comparison on observed records, not a causal model of entry and exit.

Information in this study means input to a statistical predictor. It is not a measurement of information perceived, acquired or acted on by the animals, a distinct question in information ecology (Dall et al. 2005). We likewise distinguish realized environmental use from preference, for which the individual-specific set of available alternatives matters (Johnson 1980), and predictive resemblance from genetic effects, which require a genetic design and treatment of environmental covariance (Kruuk 2004).

Thoroughbred racing is a useful managed example because the same identified horses appear repeatedly under distinct course surfaces, and the archive records direct-parent groups. Genetic studies of Japanese Thoroughbreds have examined racing times on turf and dirt, including genetic correlations between performance at different distances and surfaces (Oki 1984; Oki et al. 1997). Those studies address performance under recorded conditions. We ask instead where the horses were observed racing. Their course assignment may reflect decisions by trainers and owners, entry eligibility, scheduling, physiology and other constraints; allocation need not imply adaptation to the allocated environment.

Our central prediction is that a family-indexed correction which was useful when focal histories were sparse can overlap with subsequent focal-history updates, thereby losing incremental usefulness. We evaluate four linked questions: whether donor availability contributes to parental asymmetry (Q1); whether earlier family records add predictive value for realized allocation (Q2); whether fixed-correction gains decline within repeated horses and differ from the population trend (Q3); and whether recorded-sire direction, correction weighting and later transfer delimit the usefulness of that signal (Q4). This is an editorial hierarchy of previously conducted retrospective analyses, not a preregistered confirmatory test of all four questions. The principal longitudinal comparison is the original PR88 2022–2024 fixed-correction analysis; original analysis-specific primary tests and adverse results remain in the supplementary evidence.

## Methods

### Data, observation units and identity linkage

Source A is the SHA-pinned public Japan horse-racing archive covering 2010–2021. The broad prepared data comprise 2,092,836 classified starts from 90,962 horses. The main analysis uses only eligible JRA turf/dirt observations in the 1600–1999 m band, with recorded parent identities as required by each analysis. Source A donor and target years are 2019–2021, with earlier records used for individual-history baselines. The primary unit is a horse-year and the response is the fraction of that horse's observed eligible starts on turf. The first eligible annual start and later allocation conditional on the first observed surface form separate endpoints. The first raw record in the archive is not necessarily an official debut. Analyses of rank residuals are distinct from allocation analyses.

Source B is a separate SHA-pinned historical SQLite release, with a different native identity namespace and records through 27 March 2025. A horse could be linked to A only by an unambiguous normalized name and at least two matching dated historical race events before 2022. Historical venue codes were audited with matched events. No target-year result was used to establish identity. For 2022–2024, strict complete classified race and exact horse-ID/rank consistency criteria yielded 244,147 starts from 12,887 anchored horses across the broad panel. The narrower annual allocation target contained 3,011, 1,588 and 743 horses respectively. Both archives may originate from common official reporting sources. The linkage does not establish studbook-certified identity, and new horses without pre-2022 observations cannot receive a parent label via this method.

The 2025 seasonal analysis uses the common January 1–March 27 window in all evaluated years. Candidate race headers and native horse records are checked before the historical identity restriction, retaining counts for incomplete races, unmatched horses and earliest valid observed records. Full earlier-calendar-year histories remain available to individual-history predictions; seasonal donor profiles and weights use the matched response window. Neither the annual nor seasonal dataset supplies a complete set of feasible alternative starts for each horse.

### Individual and family prediction

For a target horse with n earlier eligible starts and turf fraction p, the individual-history predictor is b=(np+5m)/(n+5), where m is a horse-equal historical population mean for the corresponding split. Horse identities are assigned deterministically to two halves. Family residual profiles for a target half use other horses in the opposite half and strictly earlier target years. For each donor, annual allocation residuals against its historical baseline are averaged before estimating parent-group means. Let a be the global donor residual mean, q the corresponding recorded-sire residual mean and k the number of distinct donor horses. The family correction is d=k(q−a)/(k+8) for k≥3 and zero otherwise. The no-family baseline is B=b+a; the full correction is B+d. The main allocation comparison uses the same observed target horses for both models. Trainer and count-adjusted sensitivity analyses use their documented distinct baselines. Missing parents are not treated as a shared family.

To assess asymmetry in observation support, paternal and maternal donors were counted separately and sampled to the same number for each supported target. Comparisons retain the same target and count-adjusted baseline. Matched other-sire controls restrict donor-count ratios and do not use current outcomes to select a match. They cannot establish genetic unrelatedness. A management-matched sensitivity additionally restricts observed trainer, age, sex and donor year; limited support constrains this control.

### Incremental information and longitudinal decomposition

For squared error and observed turf fraction Y, the incremental gain g of adding d is

    g = (Y−B)^2 − (Y−B−d)^2 = 2(Y−B)d − d^2.

Positive g indicates lower prediction loss. When the individual baseline changes by u, holding the correction and outcome fixed,

    g(B+u,d,Y) − g(B,d,Y) = −2du.

Consequently, the mean own-update contribution factors as −2 RMS(d) RMS(u) cos(d,u), with cos the uncentered normalized inner product. This identity distinguishes magnitude and directional alignment. It holds for the actual linear scores; no probability clipping is introduced. A decline in incremental gain is not necessarily a loss of all family-associated information.

For common horses in two years, freeze d and an initial baseline B0, define u_t=B_t−B0 and let Y_t denote the observed outcome. The change is exactly

    g_late − g_early = 2d(Y_late−Y_early) − 2d(u_late−u_early).

We calculate these terms on the same horses, then subtract the common-cohort change from the change in the two full observed cohorts to obtain a membership remainder. The remainder is an arithmetic contrast, not a causal estimate of demographic survival, turnover or selection. A separate two-stratum standardization of source A2021 versus B2022 distinguishes a shift in prevalence of sparse individual history from changes in within-stratum gains; it is not the same estimand as the paired-cohort decomposition.

To distinguish recorded-sire correspondence from correction magnitude, the direction of a count-matched alternative sire's correction was applied to the magnitude of the original correction: e=|d| sign(p_swap). The primary directional contrast is 2 mean[(d−e)u], measured among matched nonzero corrections with the same earlier-to-later own-history update. Positive values mean stronger alignment of the true recorded-sire correction. This diagnostic uses the original correction magnitude and is not a separately deployable predictive model. Separate target-loss comparisons evaluate the actual value beyond own history.

### History-dependent calibration and transfer

For a common bounded weight λ, the mean gain is G(λ)=2λA−λ²E, with A=mean[(Y−B)d] and E=mean[d²]. The retrospective sample-optimal common weight is clip(A/E,0,1) when E>0; this is an explanatory diagnostic using evaluated outcomes, not a forecast. An empirical forecasting variant instead fits weights only on earlier source-A calibration years (2020–2021), using opposite-half and chronological predictions. Four fixed count bins (0, 1–3, 4–7, ≥8) define a bounded nonincreasing weight function estimated by weighted least squares with adjacent-violator pooling. Each calibration horse receives equal total weight; informative support requires 100 horses and 30 sires per bin. Monotonicity is imposed as a model restriction. Comparators are N (no family), F (full correction), P (common fitted weight), H (full correction only with zero observed eligible prior starts) and G (count-dependent correction).

In B, we separately refresh own histories and family/global residual profiles using earlier completed years. The history-dependent weights remain fixed from A. Seasonal calibration redefines earlier donor labels on the matched Jan1–Mar27 window; an annual-calibrated weight applied to seasonal profiles is retained as a sensitivity, not a replacement primary. The 2025 source coverage is not independent of the identity linkage, and B outcomes had been inspected in prior analyses. Temporal ordering of inputs is enforced, but these are retrospective tests, not a prospective trial.

### Analysis hierarchy and uncertainty

The principal longitudinal comparison uses the original primary split (seed 20261124), 1600–1999 m, and the intersection of 2022 and 2024 observed horses. The historical allocation and first-eligible-surface analyses establish the earlier predictive signal, while parent-count equalization addresses observation asymmetry. The magnitude-matched alternative-sire diagnostic, adjacent distance bands and earlier-A-only weighting assess overlap and transport rather than independent replication. For reference, the three horse split seeds are 20261124, 20261125 and 20261126.

Most contrasts use 999 recorded-sire-cluster resamples; paternal/maternal and management-matched comparisons report separate parent- or trainer-cluster sensitivities. These are conditional pointwise intervals for fixed fitted scores and observed evaluation cohorts, not uncertainty from retraining, archival linkage or all model selection. Reused horses, years and splits are dependent. Resampling by one parent does not simultaneously account for race, calendar, management and deeper pedigree dependence. When required support fails, the corresponding inference is marked HOLD rather than interpreted as zero effect. We report point estimates, intervals, distinct denominators and unfavorable results; no meta-analysis of the overlapping tests is attempted.

The supplementary material retains all 18 completed analyses and their original protocols, aggregate records and run identifiers. It includes paired performance, management, opportunity, alternative observation groups, group-specific retrospective optimal weights and the same-cohort peak nonreplication. Some diagnostic questions and the manuscript hierarchy were developed after inspecting earlier results. The later B evaluations use earlier-dated inputs, but the outcomes had already been examined in earlier studies; the evaluation is retrospective, not an untouched prospective validation.

## Results

### Donor availability and early allocation (Fig. 1)

Among 4,187 supported 2021 targets, historical paternal and maternal donor counts averaged 190.657 and 2.078 distinct horses, respectively. Equal-count sampling set both to approximately 2.071. The paternal-minus-maternal MSE-gain gap decreased from 0.008982 to 0.001258 (86.0% arithmetic reduction). Each equal-count channel improved the count-adjusted prediction in six repeated year/split conditions, but the directional gap after equalization remained uncertain under both parent-cluster intervals. The procedure addresses counts, not genetic contribution or effective independent information.

In A2021, the annual surface-fraction prediction for 7,276 horses improved from RMSE 0.423246 to 0.405495 when an earlier same-sire correction was added (MSE gain +0.014711, 95% conditional interval 0.007144–0.023246). In the first observed eligible annual surface endpoint, adding family information to a richer history/count baseline reduced RMSE from 0.403959 to 0.386565 (MSE gain +0.013751, 0.008975–0.019714). The later-surface endpoint, conditional on the first observed surface, showed a smaller point gain of +0.002085 (0.000910–0.003375, 4,477 horses). These are different endpoints and not a formal interaction test. In the separate B2022 cohort, the 334 horses without eligible source-A history had a gain of +0.019780 (0.004066–0.034388). Lack of earlier eligible observations does not establish an official debut.

The paired within-horse turf–dirt rank-residual prediction showed a much less resolved result (+0.000806 MSE, −0.001348–0.002764, 985 horses); none of twelve sensitivity intervals excluded zero. This motivates distinguishing allocation and performance, not asserting their effects differ significantly or that performance has no genetic basis. The management-matched other-parent comparison also remained uncertain on its primary 716 matched horses (Supplement S2).

### Within-horse information overlap and observation membership (Fig. 2)

For the identical 599 horses (122 recorded sires) observed in 2022 and 2024, the gain from adding the fixed earlier family correction changed from +0.011712 to −0.015866. This within-cohort change of −0.027578 decomposes exactly into the individual-history update contribution −0.016106 (−0.025057 to −0.007782; 58.4% of the signed within-cohort change) and the observed allocation shift −0.011472 (−0.021105 to −0.002387; 41.6%). The full annual cohort change was −0.017081; a positive membership remainder of +0.010497 offset 38.1% of the common-cohort decline. The remainder has no independently estimated interval. Across 18 overlapping paired/intersection sensitivities, the own-update intervals were negative and the membership remainders positive.

The 527 horses present in all three years had fixed-correction full-weight gains of +0.011493, −0.005572 and −0.017617 in 2022–2024. Their correction energy stayed constant (0.029771). Sample-optimal bounded common weights decreased from 0.693 to 0.406 and 0.204; their corresponding in-sample gains were 0.014298, 0.004917 and 0.001240. These outcomes distinguish declining residual alignment from excess weight, but the optima use evaluated labels and have no new uncertainty or forecast interpretation.

### Direction specificity and context boundaries (Fig. 3)

Among 565 matched reference-band horses (109 recorded sires), the actual and alternative-sire-direction corrections had equal RMS magnitude (0.174857). Their uncentered alignment cosines with the individual update were 0.271923 and 0.065916, respectively. The directional contrast was +0.012457 (0.004123–0.023533). All nine band-by-split estimates were positive, with eight positive pointwise intervals. Yet the actual full correction worsened 2024 prediction beyond the updated individual baseline in all nine estimates (seven negative pointwise intervals). Thus stronger recorded-family correspondence does not ensure a useful additional predictor.

The paired individual-history contribution was also negative at 1200–1599 m (−0.015038, −0.022125 to −0.008753; 531 horses). At 2000–2399 m the main estimate was smaller and uncertain (−0.001588, −0.004958 to +0.000926; 267 horses). Context-specific membership and supply differ; these are not independent populations or a formal distance interaction. The historical venue-quarter surface-supply moderator was not supported (adjusted slope per 0.1 balance +0.000441, −0.001229 to +0.002062). The within-horse moderator had insufficient support.

### Temporal transfer, calibration and coverage (Fig. 4)

After refreshing the individual and family profiles, the 2024 annual full correction increased RMSE from 0.317934 to 0.332244 (no-family minus full MSE gain −0.009304, −0.016082 to −0.001845). The history-count-dependent rule calibrated in earlier A records reduced RMSE to 0.314325; its gain relative to no-family was uncertain (+0.002282, −0.001715 to +0.006518). It outperformed full and common fitted weights in 15 later conditions, but not all comparisons to no-family were favorable.

Using the common January 1–March 27 window, the primary history-weighted rule's gains over no-family in 2022–2025 were +0.010487, +0.009225, +0.002519 and +0.000427. All three split intervals were positive in 2022 and 2023 and included zero in 2024 and 2025. In 2025 the full correction versus the history-weighted rule yielded an MSE difference of +0.010336 (0.002581–0.017463, favoring the weighted rule); its gain over no-family was +0.000427 (−0.004384 to +0.005061). The calibrated rule limits excess correction but cannot establish a reliable latest-period family increment.

Of 310 candidate 2025 races, 280 met the strict classified race requirements (3,939 starts; 2,839 native horses). Only 194 horses (6.83%; 266 starts) could be linked to the old parent archive. None of the 664 strict horses with their first valid native archive record in 2025 had a pre-2022 anchor. This is not official debut or the national registry population. Later transfer is therefore assessed on a severely selected group.

## Discussion

Our main result is not that family records sometimes improve predictions: combining related and individual records is well established in quantitative genetics. Rather, a fixed family-indexed predictor of realized environmental allocation lost incremental value as the same individuals' recorded histories were updated. Holding the correction fixed gives an exact comparison of predictor overlap. At the same time, observation membership partly concealed that decline in the full annual cohorts. The recorded-sire direction control links the overlap to the observed identity grouping under a magnitude-matched contrast, without equating a group association with inherited physiology.

This offers a prediction-centered counterpart to separating within- and between-individual change (van de Pol & Verhulst 2006; van de Pol & Wright 2009). The object that changes here is an incremental loss advantage rather than a biological phenotype or an age coefficient. An earlier rank-model comparison in this research programme showed a membership remainder of the opposite sign, although the rank within-cohort interval included zero and its estimand is different (Supplement S6). The broader implication is that the direction of membership distortion is not fixed. When a historical prediction rule is evaluated on a changing subset of a population, reporting the aggregate trend alone can obscure the within-individual trend.

The supplementary outcome-dependent weighting analysis provides a further boundary condition. An intermediate-year peak in the annual-cohort benefit of two-stratum weighting was not reproduced in the same 527 horses: all 12 overlapping point sequences decreased instead. The primary low-history group failed its support gate, and the subsequent direct-source decomposition reproduced the reconstructed subgroup moments but failed the frozen full-cell support gate in all 12 conditions. These diagnostics are useful warnings against interpreting an annual optimum as a biological time law, not confirmation of a new membership mechanism (Supplement S5).

Predictive value depends on both alignment with residual error and correction magnitude. A correction can retain family-associated structure while worsening loss if it receives too much weight. Optimal information use in a correctly specified prediction problem need not be harmful, whereas a finite, estimated or stale rule can be. The earlier-A count calibration addresses this problem with an explicit restriction on weights; the later comparisons with no-family prevent an advantage over a worse full correction from being mistaken for positive additional information. Neither the count rule nor the outcome-dependent scalar diagnostics establish a universal biological dependence on age or experience.

Two additional distinctions are necessary in interpreting this system. First, equal numbers of historical paternal and maternal donors do not equalize genetic relationships, management covariance or effective information. Second, realized allocation to turf and dirt is a managed observation, not preference, habitat suitability or fitness. The historical performance-genetics literature (Oki 1984; Oki et al. 1997) establishes why surface context matters, but it does not identify the causes of the allocation associations here. Conversely, our unresolved paired rank-residual transfer and limited management-matched comparison cannot rule out genetic performance effects. Establishing a biological mechanism would require verified full pedigrees or genotypes, stable linked identities, explicit opportunity sets and management data.

The severe later identity gap is central to generalizability. The same older-horse anchor that permits longitudinal analysis excludes many new observations, including the targets for whom we expect family information to be most useful. The prospective design should begin from a complete rights-cleared registry before a defined window, retain unknown parents and never-observed individuals, and freeze time-stamped predictions before target outcomes. No such prospective result is available. Additional retrospective selection of thresholds on these same records cannot substitute for that test.

Overall, the strongest claim is that the incremental usefulness of family-indexed predictions of managed allocation is conditional on the individual record, correction structure and observed sample. These distinctions can be applied to other repeated managed-allocation datasets, but generalization to other animals or wild habitat choice remains a hypothesis.
