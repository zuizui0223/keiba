# Individual history reshapes the predictive value of family information in managed environmental allocation

**Research manuscript v1 · 25 November 2026 · retrospective observational study · not submitted.** Author list and affiliations to be confirmed. Main text draft; four aggregate-derived figure drafts are now available. Publication-rights audit and independent reproduction remain outstanding.

## Abstract

Predictive information derived from related individuals may be particularly useful when focal individuals have little recorded history. Its additional value can change as individual records accumulate, even without a change in family identity. We investigated this process in managed Thoroughbred racing, defining realized environmental allocation as the fraction of observed classified starts on turf rather than dirt. Using earlier recorded sire and dam identities and race histories, we evaluated chronological family corrections to individual-history predictions, matched alternative-sire controls, and changes within repeatedly observed horses. In 2021, a paternal correction reduced annual allocation RMSE from 0.4232 to 0.4055 among 7,276 horses. Equalizing the number of historical paternal and maternal donors reduced the observed directional prediction gap by 86.0%, with the residual difference uncertain. In 599 horses observed in both 2022 and 2024, the incremental squared-error gain of a fixed family correction declined from 0.01171 to −0.01587. Updating individual histories contributed −0.01611 (pointwise 95% sire-resampling interval −0.02506 to −0.00778), whereas changes in observed allocation contributed −0.01147. Changing membership partly offset this within-horse decline. Magnitude-matched alternative-sire directions showed weaker alignment with individual-history updates than recorded-sire directions. History-count-dependent corrections outperformed unweighted family corrections in later evaluations, but their incremental benefit over individual histories was uncertain in 2024 and a season-matched 2025 extension. These results support experience-associated substitution of predictive information in managed allocation, rather than genetic causation or animal learning. They identify observation structure, overlap between information channels, and sample membership as distinct determinants of apparent family-information value.

**Keywords:** information redundancy; individual history; family-associated prediction; realized environmental allocation; longitudinal decomposition; managed populations

## Introduction

Ecological prediction frequently combines information about focal individuals with observations of other individuals. Family identity is one way to organize such observations. In a sparsely observed individual, relatives' records may provide useful context, but a well-observed individual may already supply much of the same information. The distinction is not merely the number of observations. Predictions depend on whether the available signals address the same uncertainty.

Information-based perspectives in evolutionary ecology motivate distinguishing information sources and their value (Dall et al. 2005). Here, information refers to a statistical predictor available to an analyst, not necessarily a cue perceived or used by an animal. We study realized allocation in a managed population rather than animal decision-making or resource preference. Observed resource use alone cannot identify preference without a defensible availability set (Johnson 1980). Likewise, a relationship between a recorded parent and offspring outcomes does not isolate additive genetic effects: shared environments, management and nonrandom allocation may contribute, and estimating genetic variance requires an appropriate genetic design (Kruuk 2004).

Thoroughbred racing provides repeated individual observations, recorded direct-parent groups, a temporally changing population and distinct managed environmental contexts. We focus on turf and dirt starts within a reference distance band. Although the system does not permit unrestricted inference about wild populations, it allows an empirical separation of three often conflated quantities: family-associated resemblance, incremental predictive value conditional on focal history, and the change in that value as the focal history is updated.

We formulate an information-substitution hypothesis: a historical family correction can become increasingly redundant with an individual-history predictor as recorded individual experience accumulates. Under a squared-loss criterion, the value of a correction depends on its alignment with errors remaining after the individual baseline and on its magnitude. Thus a correction may convey structured family-associated information yet worsen predictions when added without adjustment. This is a testable distinction between the presence of information and its incremental utility, not a claim that correctly specified optimal information use is harmful.

Four connected questions guide the retrospective analysis. (1) How much of the observed asymmetry between paternal and maternal predictive channels reflects unequal donor counts? (2) Does family history predict realized allocation beyond focal history, and is the signal concentrated in observations with sparse focal histories? (3) Can changes in the additional value of a fixed family correction be partitioned into individual-history updates, changes in observed allocation, and changing sample membership? (4) Does the history-dependent correction remain useful across alternative distance bands, seasons and later observation years, and is its overlap with individual updates specific to recorded sire identity under a matched direction control?

## Methods

### Data, observation units and identity linkage

Source A is the SHA-pinned public Japan horse-racing archive covering 2010–2021. The broad prepared data comprise 2,092,836 classified starts from 90,962 horses. The main analysis uses only eligible JRA turf/dirt observations in the 1600–1999 m band, with recorded parent identities as required by each analysis. Source A donor and target years are 2019–2021, with earlier records used for individual-history baselines. The primary unit is a horse-year and the response is the fraction of that horse's observed eligible starts on turf. The first eligible annual start and later allocation conditional on the first observed surface form separate endpoints. The first raw record in the archive is not necessarily an official debut. Analyses of rank residuals are distinct from allocation analyses.

Source B is a separate SHA-pinned historical SQLite release, with a different native identity namespace and records through 27 March 2025. A horse could be linked to A only by an unambiguous normalized name and at least two matching dated historical race events before 2022. Historical venue codes were audited with matched events. No target-year result was used to establish identity. For 2022–2024, strict complete classified race and exact horse-ID/rank consistency criteria yielded 244,147 starts from 12,887 anchored horses across the broad panel. The narrower annual allocation target contained 3,011, 1,588 and 743 horses respectively. Both archives may originate from common official reporting sources. The linkage does not establish studbook-certified identity, and new horses without pre-2022 observations cannot receive a parent label via this method.

The 2025 seasonal analysis uses the common January 1–March 27 window in all evaluated years. Candidate race headers and native horse records are checked before the historical identity restriction, retaining counts for incomplete races, unmatched horses and earliest valid observed records. Full earlier-calendar-year histories remain available to individual-history predictions; seasonal donor profiles and weights use the matched response window. Neither the annual nor seasonal dataset supplies a complete set of feasible alternative starts for each horse.

### Individual and family prediction

For a target horse with n earlier eligible starts and turf fraction p, the individual-history predictor is b=(np+5m)/(n+5), where m is a horse-equal historical population mean for the corresponding split. Horse identities are assigned deterministically to two halves. Family residual profiles for a target half use other horses in the opposite half and strictly earlier target years. For each donor, annual allocation residuals against its historical baseline are averaged before estimating parent-group means. Let a be the global donor residual mean, q the corresponding recorded-sire residual mean and k the number of distinct donor horses. The family correction is d=k(q−a)/(k+8) for k≥3 and zero otherwise. The no-family baseline is B=b+a; the full correction is B+d. The main allocation comparison uses the same observed target horses for both models. Trainer and count-adjusted sensitivity analyses use their documented distinct baselines. Missing parents are not treated as a shared family.

To assess asymmetry in observation support, paternal and maternal donors were counted separately and sampled to the same number for each supported target. Comparisons retain the same target and count-adjusted baseline. Matched other-sire controls restrict donor-count ratios and do not use current outcomes to select a match. They cannot establish genetic unrelatedness. A management-matched sensitivity additionally restricts observed trainer, age, sex and donor year; limited support constrains this control.

### Incremental information and longitudinal decomposition

For squared error and observed turf fraction Y, the incremental gain g of adding d is

    g = (Y−B)^2 − (Y−B−d)^2 = 2(Y−B)d − d^2.

Positive g indicates lower prediction loss. When the individual baseline changes by u, holding the correction and outcome fixed,

    g(B+u,d,Y) − g(B,d,Y) = −2du.

Consequently, the mean own-update contribution factors as −2 RMS(d) RMS(u) cos(d,u), with cos the uncentered normalized inner product. This identity distinguishes magnitude and directional alignment. It holds for the actual linear scores; no probability clipping is introduced. A decline in incremental gain is not necessarily a loss of all family-associated information.

For common horses in two years, freeze d and an initial baseline B0, define u_t=B_t−B0 and let Y_t denote the observed outcome. The change is exactly

    g_late − g_early = 2d(Y_late−Y_early) − 2d(u_late−u_early).

We calculate these terms on the same horses, then subtract the common-cohort change from the change in the two full observed cohorts to obtain a membership remainder. The remainder is an arithmetic contrast, not a causal estimate of demographic survival, turnover or selection. A separate two-stratum standardization of source A2021 versus B2022 distinguishes a shift in prevalence of sparse individual history from changes in within-stratum gains; it is not the same estimand as the paired-cohort decomposition.

To distinguish recorded-sire correspondence from correction magnitude, the direction of a count-matched alternative sire's correction was applied to the magnitude of the original correction: e=|d| sign(p_swap). The primary directional contrast is 2 mean[(d−e)u], measured among matched nonzero corrections with the same earlier-to-later own-history update. Positive values mean stronger alignment of the true recorded-sire correction. This diagnostic uses the original correction magnitude and is not a separately deployable predictive model. Separate target-loss comparisons evaluate the actual value beyond own history.

### History-dependent calibration and transfer

For a common bounded weight λ, the mean gain is G(λ)=2λA−λ²E, with A=mean[(Y−B)d] and E=mean[d²]. The retrospective sample-optimal common weight is clip(A/E,0,1) when E>0; this is an explanatory diagnostic using evaluated outcomes, not a forecast. An empirical forecasting variant instead fits weights only on earlier source-A calibration years (2020–2021), using opposite-half and chronological predictions. Four fixed count bins (0, 1–3, 4–7, ≥8) define a bounded nonincreasing weight function estimated by weighted least squares with adjacent-violator pooling. Each calibration horse receives equal total weight; informative support requires 100 horses and 30 sires per bin. Monotonicity is imposed as a model restriction. Comparators are N (no family), F (full correction), P (common fitted weight), H (full correction only with zero observed eligible prior starts) and G (count-dependent correction).

In B, we separately refresh own histories and family/global residual profiles using earlier completed years. The history-dependent weights remain fixed from A. Seasonal calibration redefines earlier donor labels on the matched Jan1–Mar27 window; an annual-calibrated weight applied to seasonal profiles is retained as a sensitivity, not a replacement primary. The 2025 source coverage is not independent of the identity linkage, and B outcomes had been inspected in prior analyses. Temporal ordering of inputs is enforced, but these are retrospective tests, not a prospective trial.

### Uncertainty and boundaries

Each main contrast has an analysis-specific denominator and conditional pointwise cluster interval. Most allocation contrasts resample recorded sire groups 999 times; paternal/maternal and management controls additionally report their documented separate cluster units. Sensitivity splits share data and are not independent replications. The same-horse estimates hold the sampled horse set fixed; they do not identify a causal history update intervention. We report support failures, negative gains and intervals spanning zero, rather than pool outcomes across endpoints or perform a meta-analysis of overlapping samples. Adjacent distance bands and the historical venue-quarter turf/dirt race-supply balance proxy are boundary-condition analyses, not independent datasets or a complete choice set. Detailed algorithms, exact source hashes, seeds, cohort gates and all original aggregate receipts are linked in the evidence supplement.

## Results

### Family observation support and initial allocation

Among 4,187 supported 2021 targets, the mean historical donor counts were 190.657 paternal and 2.078 maternal horses. Equal-count sampling reduced each to approximately 2.071. The full-count difference between paternal and maternal gains was 0.008982 in mean squared error, versus 0.001258 after equalization (86.0% arithmetic reduction). Each equal-count channel individually improved its count-adjusted baseline across the six repeated year/split conditions. The directional equal-count difference was uncertain under both parent-cluster intervals. This contrast indicates substantial observation asymmetry but does not identify a genetic paternal advantage.

In the primary A2021 annual allocation cohort (7,276 horses), adding earlier same-sire information reduced RMSE from 0.423246 to 0.405495, a gain of 0.014711 (0.007144, 0.023246) in MSE. For the first observed eligible surface in 2021, the richer history/count baseline declined from 0.403959 to 0.386565 RMSE after family correction; the gain was 0.013751 (0.008975, 0.019714). Subsequent allocation conditional on the first observed eligible surface had a smaller point gain, 0.002085 (0.000910, 0.003375), among 4,477 horses. In the 2,150 raw-first-record horses, RMSE was 0.446373 versus 0.406045. In B2022, the 334 horses with no eligible A history had a gain of 0.019780 (0.004066, 0.034388), with positive intervals in all three splits. Zero prior eligible starts do not imply no prior racing or experience.

In contrast, transfer of a paired within-horse turf–dirt rank-residual response had a gain of 0.000806 (−0.001348, 0.002764; 985 horses in 2021). None of its twelve sensitivity intervals excluded zero. The allocation and performance endpoints differ in response, support and baseline; their contrast motivates but does not prove allocation–performance decoupling. The primary management-matched alternative-sire contrast also remained uncertain on 716 matched horses. We cannot attribute allocation prediction to additive genetic effects or exclude shared management.

### Individual histories account for a substantial change in incremental value

Across 599 common horses (122 sires) observed in 2022 and 2024, the gain of a fixed family correction changed from +0.011712 to −0.015866. The total within-horse change was −0.027578. Updating individual-history predictions contributed −0.016106 (−0.025057, −0.007782), or 58.4% of the within-horse decline in this arithmetic decomposition; observed allocation change contributed −0.011472 (−0.021105, −0.002387), or 41.6%. The change between full observed cohorts was less negative (−0.017081) because the membership remainder was +0.010497, offsetting about 38% of the common-cohort decline. All 18 overlapping paired/intersection sensitivities had negative own-update intervals and positive membership remainders.

Among the same 527 horses observed in all three years (2022–2024), the fixed correction's full-weight gain was +0.011493, −0.005572 and −0.017617. Its mean squared magnitude was constant (0.029771). The corresponding sample-optimal common weights were 0.693, 0.406 and 0.204; the maximum sample gains attainable within that bounded common-weight family were 0.014298, 0.004917 and 0.001240. These outcome-dependent quantities describe attenuation of residual alignment in addition to over-weighting; they are not out-of-sample estimates or positive-information tests.

The separate A2021-to-B2022 two-stratum standardization attributed 79.8–89.1% of the observed gain difference to a change in the proportion of horses with eligible prior history. This comparison changes both archive and observation cohort. The within-horse result therefore adds distinct evidence: apparent population change cannot be assumed to measure only change within individuals.

### Recorded-sire direction and distance boundaries

In the 1600–1999 m primary matched subset (565 horses, 109 sires), original and alternative-sire equal-magnitude corrections had identical RMS magnitude (0.174857). Their alignment cosines with the individual update were 0.271923 and 0.065916. The directional contrast was +0.012457 (0.004123, 0.023533). All nine distance-band × seed estimates were positive; eight intervals excluded zero on the positive side. Despite stronger directional correspondence, the true full correction worsened prediction beyond the own-history baseline in all nine 2024 point estimates (seven negative intervals). This is evidence of recorded-family-associated redundancy under the tested control, not evidence of causal inherited information.

The 1200–1599 m paired own-update contribution was −0.015038 (−0.022125, −0.008753; 531 horses); the 2000–2399 m primary contribution was −0.001588 (−0.004958, 0.000926; 267 horses). The longer-distance primary interval included zero. Surface imbalance, available observations and correction magnitudes vary between bands; the differences do not constitute a formal interaction test. The historical venue-quarter surface-supply balance proxy had an adjusted slope per 0.1 balance of +0.000441 (−0.001229, 0.002062), and all twelve estimated intervals included zero. Within-horse moderator support failed. A diversity interaction for off-context family information was also unsupported.

### Later prediction and the limits of transport

For annual allocation in 2024, after refreshing own and family profiles, the full correction increased RMSE from 0.317934 to 0.332244 (MSE gain −0.009304, −0.016082 to −0.001845). The earlier-A-calibrated history-dependent correction instead yielded RMSE 0.314325, an uncertain N−G gain of +0.002282 (−0.001715, 0.006518). It outperformed the full and common-weight corrections across 15 distinct later comparison conditions, but not all gains over the no-family baseline were established.

The matched-season comparison reproduced this limitation in 2025. In the primary split, refreshed N−G gains in 2022–2025 were +0.010487, +0.009225, +0.002519 and +0.000427. All three split intervals excluded zero positively in 2022 and 2023; none did so in 2024 or 2025. In 2025, N, F and G RMSE were 0.320515, 0.335617 and 0.319849. The primary N−G interval was −0.004384 to +0.005061, whereas F−G was +0.010336 (0.002581, 0.017463); F−G intervals were positive in all three splits. Thus adaptation of the statistical weight mitigated excess correction, but it did not establish a useful additional family signal over own history at the latest horizon.

Coverage itself deteriorated sharply. Among 310 eligible candidate races in 2025, 280 met strict classified criteria, with 3,939 starts and 2,839 native horses. Only 194 horses (6.83%; 266 starts) linked to the earlier parent archive. None of the 664 strict horses whose first valid B record occurred in 2025 had a pre-2022 identity anchor. These are archive-first records, not official debuts. The latest evaluation applies to a severely selected subset, not all new entrants. A broader prospective evaluation requires a licensed stable-ID registry and dated observations before target outcomes.

## Discussion

The main contribution is a longitudinal distinction between family-associated prediction and incremental family-information value. Family histories improved prediction of realized allocation in earlier and history-poor targets, but a fixed family correction became less useful as individual histories were updated. The exact within-horse decomposition establishes that this change is not solely a result of changing cohort membership. The matched direction control connects the update term to recorded sire correspondence rather than simply to correction magnitude. In combination, these results demonstrate a statistical mechanism of overlapping predictive channels in this managed observation system.

This interpretation is deliberately narrower than a claim that horses learn where to race or inherit environmental preferences. Our model's history count indexes recorded eligible starts, and allocation reflects decisions made by people under constraints as well as attributes of horses. Stable family-specific management can create both historical family signal and later focal-history alignment. The primary management-matched comparison remains uncertain; the data do not resolve inherited physiology, shared management, population structure or their interactions. The weak evidence for paired performance transfer is consistent with an allocation signal that need not represent realized performance, but different response definitions prevent a direct equivalence test.

The analytical identity also clarifies why a family correction can carry structure and still impair prediction. The marginal value of d depends on residual alignment and a magnitude penalty. An unweighted correction may be too large for the remaining uncertainty. A historical count-dependent rule mitigates this problem in later observations, while its advantage over a no-family baseline remains uncertain in the last annual and seasonal tests. An optimal predictor allowed to ignore redundant information need not lose accuracy by receiving it; the observed worsening is a property of a finite fitted correction. The bounded scalar optimum describes evaluated data and does not establish deployable information. We do not claim a universal monotonic biological law of experience.

The parental equalization results offer a further caution: predictive asymmetry can reflect access to records rather than asymmetry in inheritance. The highly unequal paternal and maternal donor numbers make naive comparison misleading. Equalizing counts substantially reduces the observed gap but does not equalize donor relatedness, environmental independence or precision. Full-pedigree and genomic estimates would be a different investigation requiring verified identity linkage and explicit genetic modeling.

An ecological extension is to treat the value of information as conditional on observation depth and channel redundancy, and to distinguish within-individual change from changing membership in repeated ecological surveys. Such a framework could apply to managed relocation, reintroduction or other repeated environmental-allocation observations. These are proposed applications, not demonstrated generalization. We have not measured available resources at the individual level: the null venue-quarter race-supply proxy and uncertain longer-band contrast constrain attempts to explain the observed dynamics by environmental opportunity alone.

The broader research programme provides two useful, but nonexchangeable, comparisons. In earlier Japanese rank prediction, the strongly adjusted historical baseline improved from RMSE 0.304532 to 0.303338 with direct-parent histories (34,676 horses); the archive-first subgroup showed a larger point improvement (0.303661 to 0.300616). The 2022–2024 separate archive yielded a much smaller incremental rank gain, with later dependence and matched-family controls limiting the positive claim. A post-hoc 2026 factorial on 232 anchored horses showed that refreshing focal histories reduced ordinary family-model rank RMSE by 0.043402, whereas refreshing family histories alone reduced it by 0.002091; the refreshed ordinary family model still performed worse than the refreshed no-family comparison. These are separate rank endpoints, not corroborating estimates of allocation loss. Earlier rank-specific paternal and maternal donor counts were also extremely unequal (317.193 versus 2.805 under horse-equal per-start weighting), and equal-count comparisons did not establish a directional advantage. Separately, early GPS timing in a selected Australian cohort showed horse-ID repeatability and better same-horse than other-horse historical prediction, but the within-year and cross-year Doomben evaluation groups shared no eligible horse identities. This earlier identification failure motivates the same-horse design in the present study. These comparisons establish why observation depth, identity matching, and sample membership must be separated. They do not constitute independent replicates of our allocation endpoint. Detailed source-linked evidence, including partial pedigree, anonymous genomic and market-price studies that are not individually linked to the present horses, is maintained in the [repository-wide research synthesis](REPOSITORY_WIDE_INFORMATION_DYNAMICS_SYNTHESIS_20261125.md). No genomic or collective observer mechanism is inferred from those separate panels.

An earlier rank-gate comparison additionally illustrates why the sign of the membership remainder cannot be assumed. In 5,163 common 2022–2024 horses, the gate-versus-always-family loss advantage deteriorated by 0.000105406 MSE, with an interval including zero; membership amplified the observed decline to 0.000182929. In the present allocation study, membership instead offset 38.1% of the paired decline. The contrasts concern different predictive alternatives and endpoints, not a formal interaction or independent replication. They illustrate that observed cohort membership can amplify or attenuate within-horse changes. The earlier gate-selection frequency difference of −16.56 percentage points almost disappeared after standardizing on its own history-count input (+0.033 points), underscoring that a model rule and its observed exposure distribution can generate apparent group differences. See the [cross-endpoint arithmetic audit](INFORMATION_MEMBERSHIP_NONALIGNMENT_20261125.md). 

A major limitation is the temporal and identity selection of the evaluation cohort. Earlier-source anchors exclude the very new individuals for whom family information is hypothesized to be most useful. The 2025 audit reveals this limitation quantitatively. All of the retrospective tests reuse overlapping records; neither multiple splits nor multiple related models establish independent biological replication. Chronological features restrict leakage of future outcomes but do not prove the historical time at which an analyst actually possessed the records. Prospective testing should freeze a complete authorized registry before a new observation window, retain unknown-parent and never-observed horses, and commit forecasts before evaluating results. A fictional software rehearsal and draft design exist separately, not actual prospective evidence. Publication rights and data provenance must be resolved before submission.

In conclusion, the data support history-associated redundancy of family-indexed predictive information under specific retrospective comparisons. Its practical incremental value is strongest in earlier sparse-history settings, weakens with individual-history updates in common horses, and is not robustly established in the latest time windows. Observed information availability, predictor overlap and sample membership must be separated before interpreting family-associated prediction as a biological mechanism.
